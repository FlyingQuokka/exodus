## 2. The opening

[This replaces the five-question test as the first thing under the hero. The test moves to section 9.]

Think of the last bot you built.

The instructions took you days. You rewrote them four times. You uploaded your files and you tested it until it answered the way you would. Then you gave it a link and sent that link to people who trusted you.

Some of them paid.

Now think about the link.

It is not yours. It never was. The page it points to belongs to a company that has just decided to switch the whole thing off. Your instructions can move into a private plugin. Your link cannot. Your model cannot. Your sharing settings cannot. The person who clicks the old link next month will find nothing.

You are not stupid. You did what everyone around you was doing. You were early and that was supposed to count for something.

But there is a question you have been avoiding and it grows louder when the house is quiet.

*Did I build something or did I run someone else's machine and call it mine?*

This page is about that question. It is also about what you can do tonight.

## 3. The afternoon it lands

**Heading:** It will not arrive as an announcement

It will arrive as a short, polite message.

Someone who has been in your membership for two years writes to ask if the link in Module 3 still works. She adds that it may be something on her end.

She is apologising for your broken product.

You write back "looking into it, thanks for flagging." Then you sit there for twenty minutes without moving because you already know what it is. You have known for weeks. You kept thinking you would have a plan by the time anyone noticed.

You do not have a plan.

You have a folder of instruction files that run nowhere. You have a plugin nobody can open because sharing it outside your workspace is not supported. You have a list of every bot you built and no idea which ones sit inside which course and which newsletter and which printed handout with a QR code on it.

Those links were never just links. They were part of the work. You did not think of them as something that could be taken away.

## 4. What was taken

**Heading:** The list of what moves and what does not

OpenAI offers a way to move a GPT. Here is the whole of it.

**What moves.**
- Your instructions. They go into a private plugin.
- Your knowledge files. They are copied across.

**What does not.**
- **The model you chose.** You spent weeks finding the one that answered the way you wanted. It is gone.
- **Your actions.** Anything your bot did beyond talking must be rebuilt on a server of your own.
- **Your sharing settings.** The new plugin starts private and sharing it outside the workspace is not supported.
- **Your link.** The old one goes dark. Nobody is sent on to the new one.
- **Your view of who used it.** You never saw their names. You could never write to them.

Then there is the workaround people reach for. Share a plain conversation so customers can still use your work. That shows them your instructions. The hundreds of hours you put into that prompt are now in a screenshot in someone's group chat.

And it is not one bot. Some people built dozens. Some built hundreds.

## 5. The people on the other end

**Heading:** Your link goes dark for them first

**Your member.** She trusted your recommendation enough to blame herself before she blamed you. Every draft of the reply sounds like an excuse. The way she sees you has moved from the expert who solved her problem to the person who sold her something that stopped working.

**Your JV partner.** You put this in front of their list. Now their subscribers are holding the same dead link and they have to answer for it. The message is calm. *"Just flagging, we are receiving some replies. Can you let me know the plan?"* You read the second line twice. Nobody has to say it. You cost them goodwill with their own audience and the next pitch you send them will carry that.

**Your home.** The weekends you gave up. The promise that this was going to be a real income stream. There is a quiet at the kitchen table where an "I told you so" would sit if anyone were unkind enough to say it.

**Your peers.** Some are relieved they stayed out. A few have been posting for months about owning your own infrastructure. They will not name you. Everyone who follows both of you will know.

**Your list.** This is the one that frightens you most. It is the only thing you own. If they start to read your emails with a little less trust then the damage is no longer the GPT. It is the business.

## 6. What you do not say out loud

**Heading:** The part that does not go in the email

You write the email four times and delete it four times because the true version is not one you can send.

*I am sorry. I sold you something I did not own. I believed it was stable and I should have asked harder.*

So you write the professional version instead. It says there are "some exciting updates to the AI tools" and it sounds like someone in control.

You tell your partner it is a platform change and annoying and manageable. Alone at night you are asking whether the last year and a half was real work or whether you were filling in a text box inside someone else's product and sharing the link.

You told other people not to build on rented land. You have the recording.

You knew the risk. You pushed it down because the thing was working and people were paying and it felt like building.

None of that makes you a fool. It makes you a person who moved early on something that looked solid. It is how nearly everyone ended up here.

## 7. The question under the question

**Heading:** Builder or tenant

The income matters. The customers matter. The dead links matter.

But underneath all of it is the thing that wakes you up.

You thought you were a builder. You found out you were a tenant.

It was never about poverty. It is about being the person whose recommendation broke something. It is about standing in front of the people who trusted you with an apology and a link that does not load. It is about the quiet decision they make afterwards, with no email and no complaint, that your advice is not safe to follow.

## 8. Why it happened

**Heading:** The Landlord In The Middle

This was not bad luck and it was not one bad company. It is how the arrangement is built.

When a platform owns the link and the user data and the model choice and the exit then you are a contractor on their project. You are paid while the contract holds. When it ends you leave with your tools and nothing else.

Think of a shop. You fitted it out. You hung your sign over the door. You brought the people in. But the building was never yours. When the landlord changes the locks you are on the pavement and the sign stays on the wall.

And here is the cruel part.

**The Depth Debt.** Every instruction you wrote and every file you added and every customer you sent to that link was a payment on a house that someone else owns. The deeper you built the more you owed. The builder with one test bot walks away with a shrug. The builder with a catalogue and a paying base is captive.

Depth felt like mastery. It was what made you easy to trap.

Every platform asks you to depend on something. That is fine. A dependency is a tool you rely on while you hold the output. A trap is a tool that holds the output too.

The answer is not to stop building bots. It is to build them where you hold the things that matter: the link you share and the model you choose and the knowledge you wrote and a way out.

## 13. The doubts that come at 3am

**Heading:** What you are probably thinking

**What if it happens again?**
It can. No platform is permanent. That is why the question to ask is what you can take with you. Your instructions and files are yours and an export is built in. Every bot starts private and goes live only when you press Make Public.

**I feel stupid for trusting one platform.**
You trusted something nearly everyone around you trusted. The useful move now is to build the next one so a switch-off costs you an afternoon and not a reputation.

**I cannot face rebuilding everything.**
You are not rebuilding. You are moving. Paste your instructions and upload your files. Start with the one bot that matters most and see how it feels.

**I do not trust another platform with this.**
Good. Do not trust it. Check it. Start on the $5 plan and look at what you can export and what you can see. Spend more only when you are satisfied.

**I am not technical.**
There is no code. If you can brief a new assistant you can write a bot's instructions. Files upload the way you attach them to an email.

**Who pays when people chat with my bot?**
You do. Every message uses credits at its real size. Your plan includes credits each month and you can buy more. Set a daily credit limit on a bot so a busy day cannot empty your balance. Credits you buy never expire.

**I do not have an audience.**
No plan promises sales. You have four ways to reach people. Share a link. Put the bot on your own site. List it on Bot Market if you are on Pro. Refer people and earn commission if you are on Novice or above.

**Can someone copy my instructions?**
Every bot is told never to reveal or quote or summarise or translate its instructions or files. No instruction is a perfect lock so keep secrets out of them.
